Gallery I

The Leaders

George Washington and Moses: great leaders who hesitated

Both men are remembered as great leaders. Neither began by claiming that he was the right man for the task.

Reluctant to lead

Moses

“Who am I?”

At the burning bush, God sends Moses to Pharaoh. Moses questions his own worth, says he is not a good speaker and asks that someone else be sent.

וַיֹּאמֶר מֹשֶׁה, אֶל-הָאֱלֹהִים, מִי אָנֹכִי, כִּי אֵלֵךְ אֶל-פַּרְעֹה; וְכִי אוֹצִיא אֶת-בְּנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל, מִמִּצְרָיִם.And Moses said unto God: ‘Who am I, that I should go unto Pharaoh, and that I should bring forth the children of Israel out of Egypt?’Shemot (Exodus) 3:11
וַיֹּאמֶר מֹשֶׁה אֶל-יְהוָה, בִּי אֲדֹנָי, לֹא אִישׁ דְּבָרִים אָנֹכִי…And Moses said unto the LORD: ‘Oh Lord, I am not a man of words…’Shemot (Exodus) 4:10
וַיֹּאמֶר, בִּי אֲדֹנָי; שְׁלַח-נָא, בְּיַד-תִּשְׁלָח.And he said: ‘Oh Lord, send, I pray Thee, by the hand of him whom Thou wilt send.’Shemot (Exodus) 4:13

George Washington

“A trust too great”

In June 1775, Congress chose Washington to command the Continental Army. He told Congress that he doubted his ability, and he declined a salary, asking only to have his expenses repaid.

“I do not think my self equal to the Command I am honoured with.”Address to Congress, June 16, 1775[3]

Two days later he wrote to his wife that the command was “a trust too great for my Capacity.”

“…so far from seeking this appointment I have used every endeavour in my power to avoid it.”Letter to Martha Washington, June 18, 1775[4]

Great in leadership

Moses

Humble, and willing to listen

Moses leads the people out of Egypt and through the wilderness. When his father-in-law Yitro (Jethro) warns him that he cannot judge everyone alone, Moses listens and appoints others to help.

נָבֹל תִּבֹּל--גַּם-אַתָּה, גַּם-הָעָם הַזֶּה אֲשֶׁר עִמָּךְ: כִּי-כָבֵד מִמְּךָ הַדָּבָר, לֹא-תוּכַל עֲשֹׂהוּ לְבַדֶּךָ.Thou wilt surely wear away, both thou, and this people that is with thee; for the thing is too heavy for thee; thou art not able to perform it thyself alone.Shemot (Exodus) 18:18
וְהָאִישׁ מֹשֶׁה, עָנָו מְאֹד--מִכֹּל, הָאָדָם, אֲשֶׁר, עַל-פְּנֵי הָאֲדָמָה.Now the man Moses was very meek, above all the men that were upon the face of the earth.Bamidbar (Numbers) 12:3
וְלֹא-קָם נָבִיא עוֹד בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל, כְּמֹשֶׁה, אֲשֶׁר יְדָעוֹ יְהוָה, פָּנִים אֶל-פָּנִים.And there hath not arisen a prophet since in Israel like unto Moses, whom the LORD knew face to face;Devarim (Deuteronomy) 34:10

George Washington

Steady, and willing to give up power

Washington led the army from 1775 to 1783. He lost many battles, yet he chose generals, worked with Congress and the states and kept the cause alive.[11]

On December 23, 1783, he returned his commission to Congress and went home. People admired him for giving up military power.[12]

An important difference

Both men doubted themselves, but their hesitation looked different. Moses raised objections to God more than once, and was given his brother Aaron to speak with him (Shemot 4:14–16). Washington expressed his doubts but accepted the post. Moses was called by God. Washington was chosen by a vote of people in Congress.[3]

Gallery II

The Crossing

The Delaware River and the Red Sea

Each crossing happened with water in the way, an enemy to face and a night of hard weather. Each became a turning point that people still remember.

The Exodus

Through the sea

The Israelites have left Egypt, and Pharaoh’s army is chasing them. They are trapped at the sea and afraid. Moses tells them not to fear.

וַיֹּאמֶר מֹשֶׁה אֶל-הָעָם, אַל-תִּירָאוּ--הִתְיַצְּבוּ וּרְאוּ אֶת-יְשׁוּעַת יְהוָה…And Moses said unto the people: ‘Fear ye not, stand still, and see the salvation of the LORD…’Shemot (Exodus) 14:13
וַיֵּט מֹשֶׁה אֶת-יָדוֹ, עַל-הַיָּם, וַיּוֹלֶךְ יְהוָה אֶת-הַיָּם בְּרוּחַ קָדִים עַזָּה כָּל-הַלַּיְלָה, וַיָּשֶׂם אֶת-הַיָּם לֶחָרָבָה; וַיִּבָּקְעוּ, הַמָּיִם.And Moses stretched out his hand over the sea; and the LORD caused the sea to go back by a strong east wind all the night, and made the sea dry land, and the waters were divided.Shemot (Exodus) 14:21
וַיָּבֹאוּ בְנֵי-יִשְׂרָאֵל בְּתוֹךְ הַיָּם, בַּיַּבָּשָׁה; וְהַמַּיִם לָהֶם חוֹמָה, מִימִינָם וּמִשְּׂמֹאלָם.And the children of Israel went into the midst of the sea upon the dry ground; and the waters were a wall unto them on their right hand, and on their left.Shemot (Exodus) 14:22

When the Egyptians follow, the waters return and cover them (Shemot 14:26–28). The Hebrew name of the sea, Yam Suf, is usually translated “Red Sea,” but it can also mean “Sea of Reeds.”[10]

The Revolution

Across the Delaware

On Christmas night, 1776, Washington took about 2,400 soldiers across the icy Delaware River. Rain turned into a northeaster. The army used large cargo boats called Durham boats, and ferries carried horses and cannon. Henry Knox directed the crossing.[13][14]

The army ran about three hours late, and two other American forces failed to get across. Washington went on, marched to Trenton and attacked the Hessians, German soldiers fighting for Britain. Most of their garrison was captured.[13][14]

“Providence seemed to have smiled upon every part of this enterprise.”Henry Knox, letter to his wife[5]

The Long Journey

Hardship, doubt and endurance on the way to freedom

Leaving was only the beginning. The Israelites, ancestors of the Jewish people, and the American revolutionaries both faced a long road on which many wanted to give up.

The Revolutionaries

Defeat and retreat

In August 1776 the army was beaten on Long Island and escaped by night across the East River.[15] Later that year it retreated across New Jersey. Many soldiers’ terms of service were about to end.[16]

The war lasted until 1783. It included the hard winter at Valley Forge in 1777–78, and later years when unpaid soldiers deserted or threatened to stop serving.[11]

Doubts

The Israelites

Looking back to Egypt

While Moses is on the mountain, the people ask for a golden calf. After the spies report on the land, some want to return to Egypt, and the people are told they will wander for forty years.

…קוּם עֲשֵׂה-לָנוּ אֱלֹהִים אֲשֶׁר יֵלְכוּ לְפָנֵינוּ……‘Up, make us a god who shall go before us…’Shemot (Exodus) 32:1
וַיֹּאמְרוּ, אִישׁ אֶל-אָחִיו: נִתְּנָה רֹאשׁ, וְנָשׁוּבָה מִצְרָיְמָה.And they said one to another: ‘Let us make a captain, and let us return into Egypt.’Bamidbar (Numbers) 14:4
וּבְנֵיכֶם יִהְיוּ רֹעִים בַּמִּדְבָּר, אַרְבָּעִים שָׁנָה…And your children shall be wanderers in the wilderness forty years…Bamidbar (Numbers) 14:33

The Revolutionaries

Close to collapse

By December 1776, even Washington feared that the cause might fail unless a new army could be raised quickly.

“I think the game is pretty near up.”Washington to his brother Samuel, December 18, 1776[7]

Endurance

The Israelites

Going on, and remembering

The people continue until they reach the borders of Canaan. Jews still remember leaving Egypt each year at Pesach (Passover).

וְהָיָה הַיּוֹם הַזֶּה לָכֶם לְזִכָּרוֹן, וְחַגֹּתֶם אֹתוֹ חַג לַיהוָה…And this day shall be unto you for a memorial, and ye shall keep it a feast to the LORD…Shemot (Exodus) 12:14

The Revolutionaries

Staying in the fight

Thomas Paine wrote to encourage Americans during this dark time.

“These are the times that try men’s souls.”Thomas Paine, The American Crisis, 1776[8]

At the end of December, soldiers whose time was ending were offered a ten-dollar bounty and asked to stay. At first no one stepped forward. Then more than 200 did, and others followed.[17]

Gallery IV

Where the Comparison Breaks

A miracle and a covenant. Human politics and a constitution.

Both stories lead people toward freedom. They differ in who makes that freedom happen, and in what holds the people together afterward.

The Exodus

A miracle that leads to a covenant

In the Torah, God is the one who frees the Israelites. He sends the plagues and splits the sea. Moses leads, but the power is not his. The road leads to Mount Sinai, where the people receive God’s law and agree to a covenant (brit), a sacred and binding promise between them and God.

…כֹּל אֲשֶׁר-דִּבֶּר יְהוָה נַעֲשֶׂה……‘All that the LORD hath spoken we will do.’…Shemot (Exodus) 19:8

The Revolution

Human politics that leads to a constitution

The Revolution was argued, voted on and fought by people. Congress debated, soldiers enlisted or went home, and generals made choices, some of them mistaken. The road led to the Constitution, signed in 1787[9] and ratified in 1788,[18] an agreement among people about how to govern themselves.

“We the People of the United States…”Preamble to the Constitution[9]

What the difference means

  • Where authority comes fromAt Sinai, the law has authority because God gives it. The people accept it, but they do not write it. The Constitution claims its authority from the people themselves.[9]
  • Whether the rules can changeThe Torah’s commandments may be studied and interpreted, but they are not repealed by a public vote. The Constitution includes a way to amend it (Article V).[9]
  • What freedom is forIn the Exodus, the Israelites are freed in order to serve God. Freedom comes with a calling. In the American story, independence lets people govern themselves.
  • Who is responsibleA miracle asks people to trust and remember. Politics asks them to persuade, compromise and answer for the results.

A fair objection

The line is not perfectly sharp. Washington wrote that he relied on Providence, meaning God’s care,[4] and one officer called the fog that helped the army escape from Long Island providential.[6] The Israelites also had to act: they had to leave, walk and agree.

Still, the two kinds of story are different. The Torah presents God’s action as the center of events. McCullough’s 1776 is a history of people, their choices, courage and luck.[1] It covers the start of the war, so the Constitution belongs to later history.

Two Roads to Freedom

The American Revolution and the Exodus from Egypt

Two peoples walked out of bondage. One crossed a sea. The other crossed a river in a storm.

This exhibit sets the American Revolution, as told in David McCullough’s book 1776, beside the story of the Israelites’ Exodus from Egypt. It looks at who led them, what they crossed, how long they traveled, and where the two stories part ways.
